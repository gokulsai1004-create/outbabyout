Out Baby Out

Twenty minutes, two sides, and nobody is ever out for good.

Kabaddi is tag. Kho kho is tag. Both have professional leagues in this country. This is that, with one rule those don't have — your team can bring you back.

8–16 players 2 sides 20 min no kit on foot

Two moves. That's the whole game.

Vish

Poison — you call it as you touch

Catch someone from the other side and they drop where they stand. Out of the match, in the middle of the field, watching.

Amrit

Nectar — hand on them, count three out loud

A rescue is not a touch. Keep your hand on your frozen teammate and count one, two, three where everyone can hear it. Then they're back in. No limit on how many times.

The decision you keep making

Every time someone on your side goes down, you have to choose: go and get them, or keep hunting.

Rescuing costs you. You have to cross the field, in the open, to a spot the other team already knows about — because they put someone there. Chasing is safer and it feels better. It also loses matches, because a side that never rescues just runs out of people.

That's the whole thing. Elimination tag is over halfway through and everyone knows it. Here the score swings until the clock stops, and a team three players down can still win.

How a match actually goes

0:00

Both sides spread out fast. Nothing happens for a minute and everybody is nervous.

~2:00

First catch. Whoever it is drops, sits, and immediately starts shouting for someone to come and get them.

~5:00

Someone tries the rescue. Now there's a standoff — one frozen player, one defender circling them, and a teammate deciding whether to risk it.

~12:00

One side is down three. They have to choose between defending the lead they don't have and a mass rescue.

20:00

Everyone stops where they stand. Count who's upright. Most players standing wins.

The rules, all of them

  • Frozen is frozen

    Sitting down, you can't catch and you can't rescue. You can talk. Most people find out fast that talking is half the game.

  • Your own side only

    You rescue your team. You catch theirs. You can never free someone from the other side.

  • A rescue takes three seconds, and buys three

    Counting to three with your hand on someone means you are standing still, in the open, somewhere the other team already knows about. You can be caught mid-count, and then nobody gets up. In exchange, once the count finishes both of you are untouchable for three seconds so the rescue is not instantly undone.

  • Why it is a count and not a tap

    If rescuing were free, the losing team's best move would be to stand in a tight knot re-touching each other until the clock ran out, and the last ten minutes would be a stalemate. The count is what makes hiding together expensive. Guarding a fallen player is a real tactic; camping four of them in a circle is not.

  • Agree the edge before you start

    Say the boundary out loud — this path to that tree. Step outside and you're vish.

One hard rule: on foot

No bikes, no scooters, no cars. Not to chase, not to escape, not to cross the field.

This isn't caution, it's the design. A game that rewards you for closing distance with someone at speed eventually puts a person in hospital, so that version doesn't exist — not as a mode, not as an option. Pick a ground with no traffic running through it and keep the boundary away from the road.

And before the first whistle, not after it: one adult who knows the game is happening, and one person who knows where the nearest clinic is. Twenty people sprinting on grass will eventually produce a turned ankle. Deciding what happens then while everyone is still standing costs nothing; deciding it afterwards is how a good thing gets shut down.

The frozen player keeps the log

Nobody should have to sit out to be the scorer. Whoever is frozen right now is already sitting still with a view of the whole field, so the phone lives with them and moves on when they get rescued. Four seconds a line, and everybody still gets to play.
